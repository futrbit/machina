# Trump’s Executive Order on drones unleashes BVLOS drone flights

**By THE TECHNOLOGIST**

## The Unleashed Drone Sky: How Trump’s Executive Order Sparks a Technical Shift in American Airspace For the first time since the 1980s, American airspace is being reconfigured to allow drones to operate beyond the visual line of sight. This isn’t a new capability emerging from technological breakthroughs but a deliberate policy shift—one that has been quietly unfolding since June 6, 2025, according to intelligence tracking the development. The White House’s recent executive order on drones, described by sources as seeking to “usher in an era of American Airspace Sovereignty,” has triggered a 13-day window of significant activity, marking the first concrete step toward widespread Beyond Visual Line of Sight (BVLOS) drone operations in U.S. airspace. As a technologist who watches the intersection of policy and infrastructure, this shift demands scrutiny: what does BVLOS actually mean for the technology, how does it function in practice, and what critical gaps remain? BVLOS is not a new concept but a technical reality that has long been restricted by regulatory and operational constraints. It refers to drones flying at distances where the pilot cannot see the vehicle through the naked eye—typically beyond 500 meters in most regulatory frameworks. Achieving BVLOS requires robust communication systems capable of maintaining reliable, low-latency data links over extended ranges. This often means moving beyond simple Wi-Fi or Bluetooth to more resilient protocols like VHF/UHF radio, satellite uplinks, or dedicated cellular networks. Crucially, BVLOS operations also demand sophisticated safety mechanisms: real-time collision avoidance, geofencing, and fail-safe protocols that can prevent drones from crashing or interfering with other aircraft. Historically, these capabilities were either too expensive, too error-prone, or simply unregulated for widespread commercial use. The executive order’s significance lies not in inventing this technology but in removing the regulatory barriers that have previously constrained its deployment. The intelligence indicates the order has been actively implemented since June 6, 2025, with reporting intensifying through June 19. Sources like *thedronegirl.com* and *suasnews.com* describe the move as a strategic pivot toward “American Airspace Sovereignty”—a term suggesting the government’s intent to assert control over drone operations in national airspace. *suasnews.com*’s June 7 article, “Unleashing American Drone Dominance,” framed the order as a critical step to enable domestic drone networks that could support everything from emergency response to infrastructure monitoring. *thedronegirl.com*’s subsequent pieces detail how the policy aims to shift responsibility for drone operations from individual operators to a centralized system, potentially streamlining oversight while expanding capabilities. Yet the evidence doesn’t specify *how* this technical shift will be executed. The order itself doesn’t provide the communication infrastructure or safety protocols—it merely creates the regulatory space for existing technologies to operate at scale. This distinction is vital. The executive order does not deploy new drone hardware or software; it removes legal obstacles that have previously prevented BVLOS operations. For instance, the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) has long required visual confirmation for drone flights under Part 107. The order effectively relaxes this rule for specific, approved operations, allowing drones to fly farther without direct human observation. This change could enable applications like long-range agricultural monitoring, pipeline inspections, or search-and-rescue missions that were previously impractical. But the technical implementation hinges on existing infrastructure. Drones must already have the capability to communicate securely over distance and respond to safety protocols—something the order doesn’t create but enables. The intelligence shows the policy is active, but it doesn’t confirm whether the technical systems are ready or whether the transition will face real-world hurdles. The implications are profound. If successful, BVLOS operations could revolutionize how the U.S. manages airspace, reducing the need for costly ground-based monitoring and enabling more efficient use of drones for public safety and economic activity. However, the evidence reveals significant uncertainty. The intelligence sources provide a clear timeline and policy context but lack detailed technical analysis. There is no data on how many drones are currently operating BVLOS, what specific communication protocols are being adopted, or how safety systems are being verified. The White House and the FAA are the primary stakeholders, but the sources don’t identify specific teams or technical teams responsible for implementation. This gap is critical: without understanding the underlying infrastructure, the policy’s impact on safety, privacy, and airspace congestion remains speculative. As a technologist, I see this shift as a high-stakes test of policy’s ability to align with technical reality. BVLOS isn’t just about range—it’s about trust. Drones flying beyond visual range must operate with near-perfect reliability to avoid collisions or data breaches. The order’s success will depend on whether the technical systems in place can handle the complexity without introducing new risks. For now, the evidence shows a policy change in motion, not a fully realized technical capability. The 13-day window of activity reported by intelligence suggests a trial phase, not a permanent solution. What remains uncertain is whether this policy shift will translate into meaningful operational capability. Will the FAA quickly approve the necessary technical standards? Can existing drone manufacturers adapt their systems to meet the new requirements without compromising safety? And most critically, will the infrastructure for BVLOS operations scale without causing chaos in the airspace?
